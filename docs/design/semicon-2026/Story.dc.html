Five knobs. We used one.

The brief looked simple. Take a FinFET 6T SRAM cell, make it shorter, and keep everything manufacturable through M2. One rule was fixed: lithography resolution stays exactly as it was. No height could be won by assuming the scanner prints better.

The brief also handed us five knobs. At a hackathon the instinct is to start turning them. Before changing anything, we measured. The cell was 432 nm tall. It had four fin slots at a 108 nm pitch. Four times 108 is 432. The fins set the height exactly, with zero slack, and four fins serving six transistors is already the floor for a 6T cell.

That one calculation told us four of the five knobs were worth 0 nm. So the question became: can fin pitch move if lithography can't? It can. The fins are patterned by SADP: a mandrel is printed, a 28 nm spacer is deposited on both sidewalls, and each mandrel yields two fins. Fin pitch is set by the spacer, not by what lithography can resolve.

At a 72 nm fin pitch, everything held. Then we tried to break our own result. At 60 nm the gate spacer sealed the gap between fins first, no epi could grow, and there was no source or drain. No device at all.

288 nm is not where we stopped. It is where this process flow stops.

The lesson I am taking back to SCD is the one that worked on the bitcell: find the constraint that is actually binding before spending effort anywhere else.
